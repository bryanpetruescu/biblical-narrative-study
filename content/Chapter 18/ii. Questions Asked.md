> *Questions raised during the session, with answers drawn from the teaching.*

---

### Q: Aren't fallen angels and demons the same thing? If a believer can command a demon to leave in Jesus' name, why wouldn't the same authority work against a fallen angel, or against Satan himself?

They are not the same category, and the authority believers hold does not transfer evenly between them. Fallen principalities — the angelic beings assigned to govern the nations at Babel who abandoned that commission — are a different order of being than demons, which are the disembodied spirits of the Nephilim. Demons operate under Satan's authority at the individual and local level; they require an opening or invitation to gain real ground, and commanding one to leave in Jesus' name works, because that is exactly the kind of spiritual authority believers have been given.

Fallen principalities, and Satan himself, hold a different kind of authority — one established by God over the physical and political structures of the world, not just over individuals. That authority cannot be commanded away by a believer, even one acting in Christ's name, because it was never granted to believers to revoke it. Satan can act on the circumstances surrounding a believer's life, but he cannot get into a believer's spirit without invitation — and no human being has been given standing to strip him, or the principalities under him, of the governing authority God has permitted them to hold. The distinction is not about the relative strength of Christ's authority. It is about what category of authority believers have actually been given to exercise.

---

### Q: If we don't have authority to change what fallen powers have been permitted to govern, can we still pray for personal protection — or is there any point in praying about a government, an institution, or the coming persecution under the Antichrist?

Personal prayer for protection and relationship with God is not off the table — it is the entire point. What changes is the expectation attached to it. Praying against a government will not change that government, because it operates under an authority believers were never given power to overturn. The same holds for the persecution the church will face under the Antichrist in the tribulation period: it has already been ordained to happen, and no prayer reverses that.

What prayer accomplishes instead is what Psalm 23 already describes — not removal from the valley of the shadow of death, but the presence and comfort of God within it. The purpose of prayer is to know God's will and to pray it into being, not to renegotiate circumstances that have already been settled. That means praying for the souls of the people around you rather than for political outcomes or institutional change. A church that spends its energy fighting to reverse what it has no authority to reverse loses sight of what it has actually been commissioned to do: making disciples, one person at a time.

### Q: Does the temple have to be rebuilt and sacrifices restarted before the "covenant with many" is confirmed, or is that something that happens afterward?

It is a result of the covenant's confirmation, not a precondition for it. The reasoning runs from what currently prevents temple worship from resuming at all: the Temple Mount, including the Dome of the Rock, remains under the administration of Islamic religious authorities, and the surrounding nations will not permit Jewish access to rebuild or resume sacrifices under present conditions. The covenant Daniel describes is what would change that — producing enough regional peace and diplomatic cover for the Jewish people to gain the access they currently don't have.

The confirmation of the covenant itself may not be an obviously public event; it could happen through channels that don't announce themselves clearly at the time. What follows from it — a rebuilt temple and resumed sacrifices — will be visible, because those are the two things that must exist in place for the Antichrist to interrupt at the covenant's midpoint. This is also the reason Jesus, in Matthew 24, points his disciples toward the abomination of desolation rather than the covenant as the marker to watch for: a treaty can happen quietly, but a man standing in a functioning temple and declaring himself God cannot.
