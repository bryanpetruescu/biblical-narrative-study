## "All Israel Will Be Saved": The Interpretive Landscape of Romans 11

Few phrases in Paul's letters have generated more interpretive disagreement than "all Israel will be saved" in Romans 11:26. The reading this study takes — that a future, national, ethnic Israel will come to recognize Jesus as Messiah at the close of the [[Glossary#Time of the Gentiles|time of the Gentiles]], in fulfillment of a distinct and still-unrealized promise — is premillennial, but it is worth being precise about what it is not: this study explicitly distances itself from classic Dispensationalism's claim of a hard structural separation between Israel and the church as two distinct programs, a claim its own [[Glossary#Dispensationalism|Dispensationalism]] entry identifies as unsupported by the text and directly countered by Paul's olive-tree image in this very passage — one root, one tree, natural branches and grafted branches sharing the same sap. The position taken here holds both pieces together: Israel has a future, distinct national role Scripture has not transferred to the church, and Israel and the church are nonetheless organically joined, not running on separate, disconnected tracks.

That reading is not the only one on the table. A covenantal or amillennial reading, associated historically with Augustine and represented in various forms across the Reformed tradition, tends to read "Israel" in Romans 11:26 as the true, spiritual Israel — the elect from both Jews and Gentiles gathered into the church — rather than as a reference to a future national event involving ethnic Israel specifically. Classic Dispensationalism, by contrast, affirms a future for national Israel but does so by treating Israel and the church as separate divine programs in separate eras, which is the piece of that system this study rejects. A third position, sometimes called the "special path" or "Sonderweg" view and associated with a strand of post-Holocaust Christian theology, holds that Israel remains in a covenant relationship with God secured through Abraham apart from explicit faith in Christ — a view all three of the traditions above reject, since each holds that salvation for Jew and Gentile alike comes only through faith in Jesus as Messiah, whatever they conclude about Israel's future national role.

The grammatical and contextual case for reading "Israel" in verse 26 the same way Paul uses it throughout the rest of Romans 9–11 — as ethnic, national Israel, distinguished repeatedly from "the Gentiles" in the surrounding verses — is a significant point in favor of the future-national reading this study takes. But readers encountering Romans 11 for the first time should know that faithful, serious interpreters have landed in different places on this question, and that the disagreement turns less on the plain sense of any single verse than on how the interpreter understands the relationship between Israel and the church across the whole of Scripture.

## 1948 and 1967: The Historical Return

The teaching's claim that the Jewish people existed largely without national sovereignty over the land of Israel for close to two thousand years, until two specific twentieth-century events, is well-documented history rather than a matter of theological interpretation.

Following the Bar Kokhba revolt in the second century AD, Jewish presence in the land continued in reduced form, but political sovereignty passed successively through Roman, Byzantine, early Islamic, Crusader, Mamluk, and Ottoman rule, followed by the British Mandate for Palestine after the First World War. Throughout nearly all of this span, no independent Jewish state existed in the land.

That changed on May 14, 1948, when the State of Israel declared independence following the United Nations partition plan of the previous year, and the ensuing war secured the new state's borders. Jerusalem itself, however, was divided after that war — the western part of the city under Israeli control, the Old City and East Jerusalem, including the Temple Mount, under Jordanian administration. That division ended in June 1967, during the Six-Day War, when Israeli forces took control of the Old City and the Temple Mount. In a decision with lasting consequences, Israeli defense minister Moshe Dayan chose to leave day-to-day religious administration of the Temple Mount itself in the hands of the Jordanian-appointed Islamic Waqf, even as Israel retained overall security control of the site — an arrangement that persists today and is the direct reason Jewish access to the Temple Mount for the purpose of rebuilding or resuming sacrifice remains restricted.

## The Church and the Powers: Ephesians 3:10 in the History of Interpretation

Paul's claim in Ephesians 3:10 — that the church displays God's wisdom to "the rulers and authorities in the heavenly realms" — has been read in more than one way across the history of interpretation, and the disagreement matters for how a reader understands the nature of the spiritual conflict Paul describes throughout Ephesians.

The traditional reading, held broadly across the patristic, medieval, and Reformation periods and reaffirmed in much contemporary evangelical scholarship, takes "rulers and authorities" as a reference to actual angelic and demonic beings — personal, spiritual entities exercising real influence over the structures of the world, consistent with how the same vocabulary is used in Ephesians 6:12 and in this study's broader framework of a [[Glossary#Divine Council|divine council]] fractured at Babel. A different line of interpretation, associated most prominently with the twentieth-century scholar Walter Wink, reads "the powers" less as individual personal beings and more as the "inner spirituality" of institutions and systems themselves — the corporate or structural dimension of human governments, economies, and social orders. On this reading, Ephesians 3:10 describes the church confronting the spiritual dimension of unjust systems rather than confronting individual fallen angels assigned to specific nations.

This study takes the traditional, personal-being reading, consistent with its treatment of the [[Glossary#Principalities|principalities]] and the seventy nations established earlier in the series. Readers should know that the alternative, more structural reading has significant standing in modern biblical scholarship, particularly among interpreters working in social-ethical and liberationist traditions, even though it represents a minority position relative to the historic reading of the text.

## The Temple Mount and the Question of a Third Temple

The 35-acre platform in Jerusalem's Old City known to Jews as the Temple Mount and to Muslims as al-Haram al-Sharif, or the Noble Sanctuary, is among the most contested religious sites on earth. It is held by Jewish and Christian tradition to be the location of Solomon's Temple and the Second Temple, and by Islamic tradition to be the site of the Prophet Muhammad's night journey and ascension. The Dome of the Rock, a shrine rather than a mosque, was completed in the late seventh century AD over the exposed bedrock at the center of the platform; the al-Aqsa Mosque stands at its southern end.

Since 1967, the site has operated under a arrangement often summarized as maintaining the pre-1967 "status quo": Israel holds overall security control, while the Islamic Waqf, under Jordanian custodianship, administers day-to-day religious affairs and restricts non-Muslim prayer on the platform. Jewish visits to the site are permitted under tightly controlled conditions, and rebuilding or resuming sacrifice there is not currently possible under any existing political or religious arrangement.

Anticipation of a rebuilt temple is not merely a feature of Christian end-times teaching. Within Judaism, organizations such as the Temple Institute in Jerusalem have spent decades researching, and in some cases physically constructing, ritual implements and priestly garments intended for use in a future temple, and there has been periodic public discussion within religious Zionist circles about the practical and halakhic requirements for renewed sacrifice. None of this reflects a settled political outcome or an imminent construction plan — the site's political and religious sensitivity remains one of the most significant obstacles to any resolution of the wider Israeli-Palestinian conflict — but it is the reason this study treats the Temple Mount's current status as directly relevant to how the events of Daniel 9:27 and Matthew 24:15 might unfold.
