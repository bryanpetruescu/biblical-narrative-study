> *Questions raised during the session, with answers drawn from the teaching.*

---

### Q: My Bible says "partner in the tribulation" in verse 9, not "suffering" — is that a different word?

It's the same underlying Greek word in both cases. Tribulation, in this general sense, simply means suffering — specifically, suffering at the hands of someone in power. The confusion comes from the fact that "tribulation" also functions as half of a specific, capitalized phrase — the Great Tribulation — which names a fixed future period rather than the general category of suffering. John describing himself as a companion "in the tribulation" here is not a reference to that future period; it's a description of his present circumstances in exile, using the same word Scripture uses more broadly for suffering under authority.

### Q (Audience contribution): Zechariah 4:2 describes a lampstand of solid gold with a bowl on top and seven lamps with seven pipes — what does that mean?

It's the same imagery at work in Revelation's lampstands. Picture something like a menorah: a bowl at the center holds oil, and tubes or pipes carry that oil out to each of the lamps so they can burn. The lampstand itself has one purpose — to hold the oil and the wick so the light can be produced. The oil represents the source; the lampstand exists only to channel and display it. That single purpose — holding and channeling the presence of God so that light results — is exactly what is at stake when Christ later warns Ephesus that he will remove their lampstand if they do not repent.

---

### Q (Audience contribution): Revelation 2:1 says Christ "walks in the midst of the seven golden lampstands" — that's the same language used back in chapter 1. Is that intentional?

Yes. Each of the seven letters opens with Christ describing himself using imagery pulled directly from the vision in chapter 1, and the description given to Ephesus is drawn from the very phrase used to introduce the whole vision. The repetition ties the letter directly back to the image established moments earlier: Christ is not writing to Ephesus from a distance. He is present among the lampstands, and that presence is precisely what makes the church's forsaken love so serious — it is not merely a doctrinal failing observed from afar, but a grief experienced up close.

---

### Q: Could you say there's a parallelism between the angels of the churches and the scattering of the nations — that just as God gave the nations over to fallen spiritual authorities, he's now given the church angels that oversee it in a similar way?

The parallel structure is right, though "reclaim" is too strong a word for what's happening — the church isn't being handed back through the angels the way the nations were handed over to them. The more accurate parallel is this: the fallen nations have fallen angelic powers assigned over them, contending for them or against God's purposes, exactly as Deuteronomy 32 and the divine council framework describe. The church, in the same structural sense, has good angels assigned to it — strengthening its leaders and its witness in the spiritual realm, the same way Gabriel and Michael are shown in Daniel actively contending on behalf of God's people against the powers assigned over Persia and Greece.

This does not translate into authority over earthly nations or governments — the church is not given dominion over the powers instituted on earth. But in the spiritual realm, believers are not fighting with one hand behind their back. There are angels working against God's purposes, and there are angels working for them, and the church has real spiritual backing in that fight, even though it produces no visible political power in this present age.

---

### Q: I always thought the seven churches were going to be specific churches in the future — is that not the case?

No. These are real, historically specific first-century congregations located in actual cities in Asia Minor, not predictions of particular churches that would rise up at some future point. It's true that a church could plausibly exist in ancient Ephesus again someday, the way churches rise and fall in cities everywhere — but that possibility isn't the point of the passage, and the survival or non-survival of the physical congregation at Ephesus doesn't determine what the letter means.

What matters is that this local congregation does not define or exhaust "the church" in the fuller sense. Being a member of a local congregation does not, by itself, guarantee membership in Christ's body — his true church is made up of everyone who is actually in relationship with him, everyone who is genuinely his. A local church, at its best, holds a concentration of people who do belong to him and who witness to those who do not, but the local body itself is not the same thing as Christ's church. That's why every one of these letters, while addressed corporately to a specific congregation, is simultaneously speaking to individuals — each of Christ's own children, personally.
