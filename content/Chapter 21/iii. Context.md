## The Temple of Zeus at Pergamon and "Satan's Throne"

Pergamon (the modern spelling of the ancient city) sat atop a steep acropolis in the Caicus River valley of northwest Asia Minor and was, for a stretch of the Hellenistic period, the capital of an independent kingdom before passing into Roman hands in 133 BC. The city hosted an unusual concentration of pagan religious life: a major sanctuary to Asclepius, the god of healing, drew pilgrims from across the ancient world, and Pergamon was also one of the earliest cities in the province of Asia to establish an imperial cult temple to the deified emperor Augustus, making it a center of state-sanctioned emperor worship as well.

The most striking structure, however, was the Great Altar of Zeus — an enormous stepped platform crowned with a colonnaded court, built in the second century BC to commemorate Pergamon's military victories, and famous in antiquity for its monumental frieze depicting the battle of the gods against the giants. The altar's base and enclosing horseshoe-shaped colonnade gave the structure the visual character of an elevated throne overlooking the city, which is very likely what generated its identification, in Revelation 2:13, with the place "where Satan has his throne." The structure was excavated by German archaeologists in the late nineteenth century and now stands reconstructed in the Pergamon Museum in Berlin — meaning the altar that stood as a visual claim to divine authority over the city in John's day is, today, a museum display rather than a functioning place of worship, one of the many ways these ancient cities' pagan infrastructure has outlived the cities' religious identity entirely.

## Balaam, Balak, and the Strategy of Corruption From Within

The full narrative behind the "teaching of Balaam" referenced in both the Pergamum and Thyatira letters spans Numbers 22–25 and 31. Balak, king of Moab, hires Balaam — a prophet whose reputation for effective blessing and cursing was apparently known well beyond Israel's borders — to curse the approaching Israelites before they can threaten Moab. Across four separate oracles, Balaam is repeatedly compelled to bless Israel instead of cursing them, delivering some of the most concentrated messianic prophecy found outside the major prophets, including the declaration that "a star will come out of Jacob; a scepter will rise out of Israel" (Numbers 24:17).

Direct cursing having failed completely, Numbers 31:16 reveals that Balaam is later credited with advising the Moabites and Midianites on an indirect strategy: use Moabite and Midianite women to draw Israelite men into both intermarriage and participation in pagan sacrificial meals. Numbers 25 records the result — Israelite men engaging in sexual relations with Moabite women and being drawn into worship of Baal of Peor, provoking a plague that kills 24,000 Israelites before Phinehas's intervention halts it. The strategy succeeded precisely where the direct spiritual assault had failed, which is likely why Revelation returns to Balaam as shorthand for any teaching that corrupts a community of believers from the inside through compromise rather than confronting it from the outside through force.

## Sardis and the Warning of "I Will Come Like a Thief"

Sardis's letter carries a specific historical irony worth noting. The ancient city was renowned in antiquity for the supposed impregnability of its acropolis — a steep, naturally fortified citadel that had nonetheless fallen to surprise attack on at least two separate occasions centuries apart, most famously when Cyrus the Great's forces scaled an unguarded section of the cliff face at night and took the city while its defenders assumed the position could not be approached. A city famous for having twice been captured by an enemy no one saw coming is the same city Christ warns: "If you do not wake up, I will come like a thief, and you will not know at what time I will come to you." Whether or not this specific history was in view for the letter's original audience, it is a striking match for the image chosen.
